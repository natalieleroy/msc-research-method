# Appraisal — Buck & Maheux (2026)

**Citation:** Buck, B. & Maheux, A. J. (2026) 'Psychosis risk and generative
artificial intelligence use frequency, motivations, and delusion-like
experiences: cross-sectional survey study', *Journal of Medical Internet
Research*, 28, e85038. DOI 10.2196/85038. Open access.

**Appraised** 2026-10-11 against the PubMed Central full text (PMC13003207).
**Verdict: moderate — usable as the empirical core, cite from the full text only.**

> This is the public version of the appraisal. It states the findings and the
> judgements without reproducing the paper's wording. The quoted passages are in
> the private working copy.

## Why it carries weight

The largest source in the corpus — 952 participants analysed — and the only one
producing original numbers on how people at elevated psychosis risk use
generative AI. The authors are disciplined about the limits of the design: they
state that directionality is unavailable, decline to claim harm in either
direction, and say the association could run either way or be driven by a third
factor. That restraint is the main reason the findings are citable.

The design matches the question. The question was how much, for what, and how
often — a prevalence and association question, which a cross-sectional survey
answers. It was not a causal question and the paper does not pretend otherwise.

## Two discrepancies between the abstract and the full text

Both found by reading the tables against the abstract. Both matter because the
abstract is what most readers will quote.

**1. The intensive-use claim is overstated in the abstract.**
The abstract presents three intensive-use items under a single significant odds
ratio range. Table 1 shows that one of those items — time spent per session —
was not significant: OR 1.32 (0.84-2.08), chi-square 1.42 (1), P=.23. The full
text concedes that there was no group difference on session length. The abstract
does not.

*Consequence:* cite the three items that held — frequency per day, use on the day
of survey, number of conversations per day — and name session length as the one
that did not. The honest version is also the more precise one.

**2. The abstract's group sizes are not the analysed group sizes.**

| Figure | Where it appears | What it actually is |
|---|---|---|
| 1003 | abstract | sample before exclusions |
| 952 | Results | final analysed sample |
| 846 (88.9%) | Results | of those, the participants who had ever used GenAI |
| 267 / 685 | abstract | stated elevated / low risk split (sums to 952) |
| 241 / 605 | every table | elevated / low risk split among GenAI users (sums to 846) |

The 267/685 split appears nowhere in the results and is never reconciled with the
tables.

*Consequence:* cite 952 analysed, 846 users, groups of 241 and 605. Citing "1003
young adults" as the analysed sample would be citing a number that no reported
statistic rests on.

## Weaknesses that constrain the claim

- **Cross-sectional.** Nothing here establishes that AI use affects symptoms. The
  paper's own framing — risk may drive use, use may drive symptoms, or a third
  factor may drive both — is the only defensible reading.
- **Two of three instruments are new.** The motivations scale rests on a factor
  analysis the authors describe as unpublished; the delusion-related experiences
  scale was written for this study. Internal consistency is high (alpha 0.95) but
  that is not validity, and on sixteen items it can equally indicate redundancy.
- **Prolific convenience sample**, United States only, ages 18-25. The authors
  concede it is likely more online-tool-literate than the general population.
  This biases GenAI use upward, and the direction is knowable, so it should be
  stated rather than hedged.
- **The risk threshold is provisional** and self-described as such. "Elevated
  risk" here means a screening score at or above a cut-off of 20 on the PQ-B
  distress total, which the authors say does not itself establish clinical
  high-risk criteria — that is a clinician's determination. The essay must not
  silently upgrade it to "at-risk patients".

## How to use it

As the empirical anchor of the evidence section, cited from the full text, with
the directionality limit attributed to the authors and the session-length
correction made explicitly. It cannot support any claim that generative AI causes
or worsens psychosis, and the essay should say so before the reader does.

## The most quotable thing in it is not its headline

It is that 46.5% of the elevated-risk group described a general-purpose chatbot
as their therapist, against 22.0% of the low-risk group — OR 3.08 (2.24-4.24),
P<.001. Quote the cells rather than the abstract's odds-ratio range, which
flattens it.
