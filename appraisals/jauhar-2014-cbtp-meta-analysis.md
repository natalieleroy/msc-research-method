# Appraisal — Jauhar et al. (2014)

**Citation:** Jauhar, S., McKenna, P. J., Radua, J., Fung, E., Salvador, R. &
Laws, K. R. (2014) 'Cognitive-behavioural therapy for the symptoms of
schizophrenia: systematic review and meta-analysis with examination of potential
bias', *British Journal of Psychiatry*, 204 (1): 20-29. DOI
10.1192/bjp.bp.112.116285. Bronze open access.

**Appraised** 2026-10-12 against the full text.
**Verdict: strong — the primary source for the magnitude of CBT's effect on the
symptoms of schizophrenia, and the bias gradient is the part worth citing.**

> This is the public version of the appraisal. It states the findings and the
> judgements without reproducing the paper's wording. The quoted passages are in
> the private working copy.

## Why it carries weight

The design matches the question and then exceeds it. A PRISMA systematic review
with random-effects meta-analysis across four databases, from 1993 — the year of
the first CBT trial in schizophrenia — to the end of March 2013: 1594 records
identified, 1246 after de-duplication, 90 full texts assessed, 50 articles
reporting 52 studies included. That is a complete sweep of the field as it then
stood rather than a convenience set.

What lifts it above a competent pooling exercise is that bias was a pre-specified
object of study rather than a closing caveat. The Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool was
applied to three domains — sequence generation, allocation concealment and
incomplete outcome data — with explicit rules set in advance for each, including
an attrition threshold waived where drop-outs were accounted for or
intention-to-treat was used, named criteria for evidence of central allocation,
and a requirement that assessor masking be stated rather than implied. Masking of
outcome assessment and use of a control intervention were then tested as
moderators, and publication bias was examined three separate ways: trim-and-fill,
a rank correlation test and a test of the funnel-plot intercept.

The authors are candid where candour costs them. They report that the moderator
analysis for randomisation and for incomplete outcome data could not be run
statistically because too few trials fell into the high-risk category, and they
attach the fragility of the low-risk-of-bias subanalysis to the subanalysis
itself. Declaration of interest: none. Funding: CIBERSAM and the Instituto de
Salud Carlos III, neither with a stake in the question.

## The finding is the gradient, not the headline

The headline — a small pooled effect — is the least interesting result and the
one most likely to be cited badly. The result that matters is how far the effect
moves as methodological quality rises.

*Effect sizes are negative and favour CBT. Confidence intervals at 95%; study
counts after each estimate.*

Masking moderated overall symptoms (Q(B) 10.10, *P*=0.001) and positive symptoms
(Q(B) 20.51, *P*<0.001) but not negative symptoms (Q(B) 1.27, *P*=0.26). The
bold cell is the most consequential number in the paper: across the twenty trials
in which assessors did not know who had received the therapy, the effect on the
symptoms CBT was designed to treat is indistinguishable from zero. Restricted to
trials at low risk of bias on all three domains, all three outcomes are
non-significant. The authors put the size of the masking gradient at four- to
sevenfold across the three main analyses and compare it with the 17-36%
exaggeration documented in trials of medical treatments.

Two secondary results are worth having. Use of a control intervention did not
moderate effect size in any analysis (overall *P*=0.99, positive *P*=0.68,
negative *P*=0.48), which is surprising on its face; the authors offer the
obvious explanation that nine to twelve control-intervention trials may be too
few to detect it. And the publication-bias tests disagree: the rank correlation
test was significant for overall (*P*=0.009) and negative symptoms (*P*=0.02)
while the intercept test was not (*P*=0.06 and *P*=0.30), and trim-and-fill moved
the positive-symptoms estimate by 0.01. The authors' summary — no consistent
evidence of publication bias — is a fair reading of a mixed picture, but it is
not the same as no evidence, and citing this paper for the absence of publication
bias overreads it.

## Weaknesses that constrain the claim

- **The low-risk-of-bias subanalyses rest on 8 to 11 studies**, and not because
  most trials were poor: most were rated *unclear*, reporting too little about
  randomisation, concealment or missing data to be classified either way. The
  authors state that the resulting estimates may not be reliable. These are the
  most-quoted numbers in the paper and its thinnest. Quote them with the study
  count attached or not at all.
- **Depression, anxiety and distress were never examined**, and the authors say
  they make no judgement on them. This is the limit that bites hardest on a
  formulation-led plan, which is far more likely to want CBT for distress than
  for symptom reduction. On that question this source is silent by its own
  admission.
- **The search ends March 2013.** Several large trials, a Cochrane update and a
  network meta-analysis postdate it. The pairing that covers the field is this
  paper for the magnitudes plus a later source for the subsequent trials.
- **End-of-treatment data only.** Durability, follow-up and relapse are out of
  scope.
- **Three of six authors also wrote the 2019 editorial** and two wrote a 2018
  meta-analysis of functioning, distress and quality of life. The figures are
  published and checkable and the declaration of interest is none, so this is not
  a reason to discount them; it is a reason to present these authors as a position
  within a live controversy rather than as its referee. A reader who meets only
  this source will not know that its authors are the sceptics.
- **Trial-level countries, ages and diagnostic composition were not extracted**
  into the main tables, and the online supplements holding per-study effect sizes
  and risk-of-bias ratings were not retrieved, so the recruitment countries and
  the WEIRD coding are left uncoded rather than guessed.

## A note on the citation this replaces

The 2019 editorial by the same lead authors reports these figures with the sign
reversed to positive, and states that it does so. The estimates were checked line
by line against the primary: the three pooled estimates, the masked-subgroup
estimates and the low-risk-of-bias subanalysis all match exactly. The problem
with the earlier citation was never accuracy — it was citing an editorial for a
magnitude. The editorial remains worth citing, as an editorial, for the
post-2013 trials it surveys.

## How to use it

It supports: that the pooled end-of-treatment effect of CBT on the symptoms of
schizophrenia is small; that it attenuates as methodological quality rises; and
that in masked trials the effect on positive symptoms is not statistically
significant. The authors' further inference — that claims of effectiveness
against positive symptoms can no longer be sustained — can be reported as their
inference.

It does not support: that CBT is ineffective overall, since non-significance
across 8 to 11 trials is not a demonstration of no effect; anything about
distress, depression or anxiety, which were not examined; anything about relapse,
durability or functioning, which are out of scope; or the conclusion that
publication bias was ruled out, since the three tests disagree.

The phrase "small to moderate" should not be attributed to this paper. The word
it uses is small, and it uses it deliberately.
