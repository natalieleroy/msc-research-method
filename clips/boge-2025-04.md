**Why this clip is here:** RESOLVED, but the claim has to be REWORDED, and the rewording is not cosmetic. Boge's sentence - that differences in coping ability play a role in symptom EXACERBATION - is not supportable. MacAulay & Cohen (2013) does not study exacerbation at all (cross-sectional, 27 patients, headline null, and Boge's sentence traces to MacAulay's own introduction). Strous et al. (2005) is genuinely longitudinal but runs the arrow the OTHER WAY: every regression has coping as the dependent variable and symptoms as a predictor of it, severity explaining 3.5% to 9% of coping variance, and the authors conclude coping [quoted wording withheld]. Using Strous here would reverse its own causal framing - the same error made three times already in this chain. What IS evidenced is narrower and still enough: Schlier et al. (2020) found that [quoted wording withheld], and fitted the reverse model too - improvement in suspiciousness or positive symptoms did NOT predict later coping improvement, though negative symptoms and depression did. So write coping as a MODIFIABLE MAINTAINING FACTOR whose improvement precedes symptom improvement within therapy, not as a deficit that drives exacerbation in untreated psychosis. Four limits travel with it: 57 German outpatients, one group, both measures self-reported by the same person, and individualised positive symptoms conspicuously absent from what coping improvement preceded. Also note Strous's honest use, which is real: coping is STATE-DEPENDENT, emotion-focused coping rising in acute phases - so a coping assessment taken during an acute presentation is measuring a moving target, which is a point the formulation section can use. INDEPENDENCE: Schlier's senior author is Tania Lincoln, who is also Lincoln & Peters (2019) and Lincoln & Heibach (2017), so four links in what looked like a six-source theoretical chain come from one Hamburg group.
