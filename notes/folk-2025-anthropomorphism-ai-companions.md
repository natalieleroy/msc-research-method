# Folk, D., Heine, S. J. & Dunn, E. (2025)

**Individual differences in anthropomorphism help explain social connection to AI companions**

DOI: 10.1038/s41598-025-19212-2

## How much weight it bears: moderate

Two randomised experiments, the second pre-registered and high-powered, on exactly the question Nass & Moon (2000) opened: whether people respond socially to a conversational machine. It CAN establish that a six-minute warm-prompted LLM chatbot produces more state social connection than a matched journaling control, and that the size of that gain depends on a measured individual difference — the Johnson-Neyman thresholds put 58% (Study 1) and 72% (Study 2) of participants above the point where the chatbot beat journaling, and below it there was no significant difference. It CANNOT establish that the social response is universal or mindless, which is the thing the corpus has been extending Nass & Moon to claim: the condition effect shrank from d = 0.32 to d = 0.15 in the larger study, the interaction is small (standardised 0.100, P = .013), and in Study 2 anthropomorphism predicted connection in the JOURNAL condition too (r = .310), so part of the association is not chatbot-specific at all. Also cannot bear weight on psychosis, on prolonged use, or on non-WEIRD populations: outcome is a single self-report item, exposure is six minutes, and the sample is American and Canadian Prolific workers whom the authors themselves call 'especially comfortable with technology'. Moderate rather than strong: good randomisation, pre-registration, open data and a well-chosen active control, against a one-item outcome, a brief exposure and a non-clinical convenience panel.
